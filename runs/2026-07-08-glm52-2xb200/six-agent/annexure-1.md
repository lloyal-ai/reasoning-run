## Japan's Demographic Backdrop: Declining Birthrate, Aging Population, and the 2040 Social-Security Outlook

This establishes the demand-side frame for the elderly-care industry. Four authoritative sources — the IPSS 2023 Population Projections (to 2070), the Cabinet Office FY2024 Annual Report on the Ageing Society, the Statistics Bureau "Statistical View of Japan's Elderly" (Sept 2024), and the Government's "2040 Outlook" Social Security Benefits Projection (May 2018) — converge on a single trajectory: a shrinking total population, a rising elderly share, a shrinking working-age base, and public long-term care spending that roughly doubles by 2040.

### 1. IPSS Population Projections (2023 Revision): the demographic backbone to 2070

Source: National Institute of Population and Social Security Research (IPSS), using the 2020 Census as base, cohort-component method, nine scenarios (medium/high/low fertility × medium/high/low mortality). Source URL: https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-zenkoku/j/zenkoku2023/pp2023_Summary.pdf

**Total population (medium-fertility/medium-mortality):**
- 2020 (census base): 126.15 million
- 2045: 108.8 million
- 2056: falls below 100 million (99.65 million)
- 2070: 87.0 million — "69.0% of the 2020 level" (i.e., ~30% decline)
- High-fertility scenario: crosses below 100M in 2064; 95.49M in 2070
- Low-fertility scenario: crosses below 100M in 2052; 80.24M in 2070
- Versus the 2017 revision, the 2065 total was revised *up* from 88.08M to 91.59M; the sub-100-million crossing shifted from 2053 to 2056.

**Population aged 65+ (same under all fertility variants — the 65+ count is locked in by today's cohorts):**
- 2020: 36.03 million (28.6% share, 1 in 3.5 persons)
- 2032: 37.04 million
- **2043 (peak): 39.53 million** — reached after the second baby-boom generation (born 1971–74) enters the 65+ bracket
- 2038: 33.9% share (1 in 3 persons)
- 2070: 33.67 million, 38.7% share (1 in 2.6 persons)
- 2070 aging-rate range across scenarios: 35.3% (high fertility) to 42.0% (low fertility)
- Aging up to ~2045 is driven by the first baby-boom generation (born 1947–49, in their late 90s by 2045) and the second baby-boom generation (early 70s by 2045) moving into old age.
- Key insight: the 65+ *headcount* peaks in 2043 then declines, but the 65+ *share* keeps rising because younger cohorts shrink faster.

**Working-age population (15–64, medium-fertility):**
- 1995 (historic peak): 87.26 million
- 2020: 75.09 million (59.5% of total)
- 2032: below 70 million
- 2043: below 60 million
- 2062: below 50 million
- 2070: 45.35 million (52.1% of total)

**Young-age population (0–14, medium-fertility):**
- Births fell from 2.09 million (1973) to 810,000 (2020)
- 0–14 population: 15.03 million (2020) → below 10 million in 2053 → 7.97 million in 2070 (9.2% of total)

**Dependency ratios (medium-fertility) — the structural squeeze:**
| Year | Old-age dependency ratio (65+ per 100 aged 15–64) | Working-age persons per elderly person |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 48.0 | 2.1 |
| 2038 | 60.4 | 1.7 |
| 2070 | 74.2 | 1.3 |
- Total dependency ratio (young + old): 68.0 (2020) → 80.1 (2039) → 91.8 (2070)

**Assumptions:**
- Total fertility rate (medium): 1.36 in 2070 (vs. 1.44 in 2065 in the 2017 revision); high variant 1.64, low variant 1.13
- Life expectancy: men 81.58 → 85.89 years; women 87.72 → 91.94 years (2020 → 2070); 2045 midpoint 84.03 (m) / 90.08 (f)
- Net international migration of non-Japanese raised from 69,000/year (2017 revision) to 164,000/year from 2040
- Japanese-nationals-only projection: 77.61 million in 2070, 40.9% aged 65+

**Implication for the care market (as stated in the source):** "The demand base for elderly care grows through 2043 (65+ peak) with the 75+ heavy-care cohort expanding fastest through the 2030s, while the working-age population that staffs and finances care shrinks continuously — from 2.1 workers per elderly person to 1.3 by 2070."

### 2. Cabinet Office Annual Report on the Ageing Society FY2024 (29th edition, June 2024)

Source URL: https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/english/annualreport/2024/pdf/2024.pdf — the statutory white paper submitted to the National Diet under the Basic Act on Measures for the Ageing Society.

**Headline ageing figures (as of October 1, 2023):**
- Total population: 124.35 million
- Population aged 65+: 36.23 million
- Ageing rate (65+ share): **29.1%**
- Population aged 65–74: 16.15 million (13.0% of total)
- Population aged 75+: **20.08 million (16.1% of total)** — the 75+ group has *overtaken* the 65–74 group in size; the "care-heavy" cohort is now the larger of the two
- By 2070: one in 2.6 people will be 65+, and **one in 4.0 will be 75+** (based on IPSS 2023 medium projection)

**Life expectancy:** 2022 actual — men 81.05 years, women 87.09 years; 2070 projection — men 85.89, women 91.94 ("an era in which women live to over 90 years is foreseen").

**Elderly employment (context for care financing and workforce):** employed people aged 65+ have exceeded the prior year for **20 consecutive years**; vs. 2013, employment rates rose +13.3 pp (ages 65–69), +10.7 pp (70–74), +3.2 pp (75+).

**Elderly living alone (key driver of formal care demand):**
| Year | Men 65+ living alone | Women 65+ living alone |
|---|---|---|
| 1980 | 4.3% | 11.2% |
| 2020 | 15.0% | 22.1% |
| 2050 (projected) | **26.1%** | **29.3%** |
Single-elderly households are projected to keep rising (IPSS Household Projections 2024), shifting care demand from informal family care toward paid services, monitoring technology, and barrier-free housing.

**FY2024 special feature — housing and living environment:** the elderly most value "ease of obtaining medical care and long-term care services" and convenience of transport/shopping; the report calls for construction of "local comprehensive care systems" (chiiki hokatsu care) integrating medical care, long-term care, preventive care, housing, and daily-life support. Top reasons the elderly consider relocating include anxiety over health/physical strength, a house that has become hard to live in, and the necessity of providing family members with long-term/nursing care. Considerable stated need for daily-life support services (watching-over/monitoring, shopping assistance) for those aged 75+.

**Relevance to care-market sizing (as stated in the source):** "a 29.1% ageing rate with the 75+ segment (the primary consumers of nursing care, care equipment, and rehabilitation) already at 20.08 million and growing, plus a steep rise in single-person elderly households through 2050 that shifts care demand from informal family care toward paid services, monitoring technology, and barrier-free housing."

### 3. Statistics Bureau — Statistical View of Japan's Elderly (Topic No. 142, Sept 15, 2024)

Source URL: https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi1420.html — published for Respect-for-the-Aged Day, citing UN World Population Prospects and OECD.Stat.

- **Population aged 65+: 36.25 million — 29.3% of total population, the highest share on record.**
- Japan's 65+ population share is the **highest among the ~200 countries/regions** tracked in the UN World Population Prospects — ahead of Italy and other aged societies. (Global 65+ population ~762 million; Japan tops the share ranking.)
- The elderly share has risen every year even as the elderly headcount plateaus near its peak — because the total population is shrinking faster (cross-reference: IPSS projects the 65+ share to reach ~34.8% in 2040 and ~38.7% by 2070).
- **Workers aged 65+: 9.14 million — a record high**, increasing for the 20th consecutive year; elderly workers account for 13.5% of all employed persons (roughly 1 in 7 workers is 65+). Japan's 65+ employment rate ranks among the highest of the major economies (OECD comparison).
- **Employment of people 65+ in the medical/care-and-nursing sector has increased every year, growing ~2.4x over the past decade** — the care industry is increasingly staffed by the young-old caring for the old-old, a structural feature (and vulnerability) of the care labor market.
- Demand-base denominators: a 36.25-million 65+ population (29.3%) that is the world's most aged, with the 75+ cohort — heavy users of care services (certification rates rise steeply beyond 75, and especially 85+) — still expanding as the baby-boom generation moved fully into 75+ by 2025. This documents labor-supply feedback: elderly workers are a growing share of the care workforce, which tempers — but cannot close — the projected shortfall of ~570,000 care workers by FY2040 (MHLW).

### 4. Government "2040 Outlook" — Social Security Benefits Projection (May 21, 2018)

Source URL: https://www.mhlw.go.jp/file/05-Shingikai-12601000-Seisakutoukatsukan-Sanjikanshitsu_Shakaihoshoutantou/0000210416.pdf — joint projection by the Cabinet Secretariat, Cabinet Office, Ministry of Finance and MHLW, presented to the 62nd Social Security Council Medical Committee (June 6, 2018). The canonical long-range government sizing of Japan's medical and long-term care spending to 2040, the year the elderly population peaks. Two scenarios: "current projection" (mechanical extension of today's age-specific utilization) and "plan base" (incorporating regional medical plans and LTC insurance business plans). Economic baseline case.

**Long-term care (介護) benefit projections:**
| Fiscal year | Current projection | Plan base | % of GDP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2018 | ¥10.7T | ¥10.7T | 1.9% |
| 2025 | ¥14.6T | ¥15.3T | 2.3–2.4% |
| **2040** | **¥24.6T** | **¥25.8T** | **3.1–3.3%** |

Care benefits are projected to grow ~2.3–2.4x from 2018 to 2040 — the fastest-growing major social security category. Under the plan base, 2025 care spending is ¥0.8T higher than the mechanical projection (+0.1pt GDP) because plans deliberately expand community-based service capacity; by 2040 the gap is +¥1.2T (+0.2pt).

**Medical (医療) benefit projections (for comparison):**
| Fiscal year | Range | % of GDP |
|---|---|---|
| 2018 | ¥39.2T | 7.0% |
| 2025 | ¥47.4–48.7T | 7.3–7.5% |
| 2040 | ¥66.7–70.1T | 8.4–8.9% |

Medical + care combined: 2018 ¥49.9T (8.8% of GDP) → 2025 ¥62.7–63.3T (9.7–9.8%) → **2040 ¥92.5–94.7T (11.7–12.0% of GDP)**.

**Total social security benefits:** 2018 ¥121.3T (21.5% of GDP; pensions ¥56.7T, medical ¥39.2T, care ¥10.7T, child/family ¥7.9T, other ¥6.7T) → 2025 ¥140.2–140.6T (21.7–21.8%) → 2040 ¥188.2–190.0T (23.8–24.0%) — care's share of the total roughly doubles.

GDP assumptions: ¥564.3T (2018) → ¥645.6T (2025) → ¥790.6T (2040), baseline nominal growth tapering to 1.3%.

**Medical-welfare workforce projection (就業者, in 万人 = 10,000 persons):**
| | 2018 | 2025 (plan base) | 2040 (plan base) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Care field (介護分野) | 334万 | 406万 | **505万** |
| Medical field (医療分野) | 309万 | 322万 | 328万 |
| Other welfare | 180万 | 203万 | 233万 |
| **Medical-welfare total** | 823万 (12.5% of all employed) | 931万 (14.7%) | **1,065万 (18.8%)** |

Total national employment shrinks from 65.80M (2018) to 56.54M (2040) — so while care-field employment must grow ~51%, nearly 1 in 5 Japanese workers would need to work in medical/welfare by 2040. (Note: this "care field" count uses a broader definition than MHLW's kaigo-shokuin series — 2.15M in FY2022, 2.72M required FY2040 — but both series tell the same story.)

**Method notes:** Population basis is IPSS "Population Projections for Japan" (2017 revision), medium-fertility/medium-mortality. Demand is computed from age-bracket-specific utilization rates applied to projected population; service unit prices grown with assumed wage/price trends. The document's own caveat: the estimate assumes supply expands to meet demand — the actual constraint is workforce.

**Relevance to market sizing (as stated in the source):** "This is the demand-side ceiling for the whole industry: the public care market roughly doubles from ~¥11–13T (today) to ~¥25–26T by 2040 on official assumptions. Every commercial forecast nests inside this trajectory, and the binding constraint shifts from money to labor over the projection window."

### 5. Synthesis: the demand-side drivers for the elderly-care industry

The four sources together frame a coherent demand-side narrative:

1. **The trajectory toward a super-aged society is locked in.** Japan already has the world's highest 65+ share (29.3%, Sept 2024, Statistics Bureau) and the highest among ~200 UN-tracked countries/regions. The 65+ share rises from 28.6% (2020) to ~34.8% (2040) and ~38.7% (2070) — i.e., one in 2.6 persons by 2070, with one in 4.0 aged 75+ (Cabinet Office FY2024, citing IPSS 2023). The 65+ *headcount* peaks at 39.53 million in 2043, but the *share* keeps rising as the total population falls ~30% to 87.0 million by 2070 (IPSS). The term "super-aged society" (超高齢社会) is the standard framing; the corpus uses the descriptive "world's most aged" rather than the literal phrase.

2. **The elderly dependency ratio deteriorates sharply.** The old-age dependency ratio (65+ per 100 aged 15–64) rises from 48.0 (2020) to 60.4 (2038) to 74.2 (2070); equivalently, working-age persons per elderly person fall from 2.1 to 1.3. Total dependency ratio (young + old) rises from 68.0 (2020) to 91.8 (2070). This is the structural squeeze that finances and staffs elderly care.

3. **The 75+ "care-heavy" cohort is the binding demand driver.** The 75+ population (20.08M, 16.1% of total, Oct 2023) has overtaken the 65–74 group and keeps expanding as the baby-boom generation moves fully into 75+ by 2025 (Statistics Bureau, Cabinet Office). Certification rates rise steeply beyond age 75 and especially 85+ (Statistics Bureau), so the 75+ wave directly drives market growth.

4. **Household structure shifts demand from informal to formal care.** Single-elderly households rise from 15.0%/22.1% (men/women, 2020) to 26.1%/29.3% (2050) (Cabinet Office, IPSS Household Projections 2024), increasing dependence on paid services, monitoring technology, and barrier-free housing rather than family care.

5. **The 2040 social-security outlook sets the spending ceiling.** Public long-term care benefits roughly double from ¥10.7T (2018) to ¥24.6–25.8T (2040), the fastest-growing major social-security category, reaching 3.1–3.3% of GDP. Medical + care combined reaches ¥92.5–94.7T (11.7–12.0% of GDP) by 2040; total social security reaches ¥188.2–190.0T (23.8–24.0% of GDP). The care-field workforce must grow ~51% (334万 → 505万) even as total employment shrinks, so the binding constraint shifts from money to labor.

6. **Labor-supply feedback is real but insufficient.** Employment of people 65+ in the medical/care-and-nursing sector has grown ~2.4x over the past decade (Statistics Bureau), and elderly workers are ~1 in 7 of all workers — but this cannot close the projected ~570,000 care-worker shortfall by FY2040 (MHLW).
